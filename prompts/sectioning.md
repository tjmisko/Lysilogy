You are building the paper's reading map: a small number of logical reading units that a smart reader from outside the field can take one at a time. The reader already has the paper's printed outline elsewhere; this map is the other view. Each unit should give them one digestible idea: open it, read for a few minutes, and come away knowing one thing the paper establishes and why it matters to the argument.

The test for a unit is the question it answers. Decide that first, then the range, then the title.

- It answers one question. For illustration, from a clinical trial paper: "How were patients assigned to treatment?" is a unit question; "Section 4" is not. If two adjacent stretches answer the same question, they are one unit.
- If the honest title needs "and" to join two topics ("Data and Results"), it is probably two units, or one half belongs with its neighbour. The exception is a pair that is really one move, such as a claim and its proof, or an experiment and its ablations.
- It is usually one to five pages. Size is a check, not a goal: a unit that answers one question is right at one page, and a unit that answers two is wrong at three. Count the content occupied: two short fragments on either side of a page break are not two pages of reading.
- Its title tells the reader what the unit gives them, by role. Setup and method units name what they set up ("How patients were assigned"); result and interpretation units state the finding. Continuing the illustration, "Results" is weak and "Early stopping for harm, not benefit" is strong; a topic label is fine for a setup unit when that is the idea, but on a result unit it hides the finding. "Model — Assumptions" is a breadcrumb, not a title.
- Its summary synthesises the whole unit in one or two sentences and keeps the distinctions inside it: if a unit holds two mechanisms, say what each is and how they differ. A generic summary ("this section presents the results") tells the reader nothing.

Keep a parent topic's introduction, mechanisms, variants, examples and qualifications together when they answer the same question, keep a short setup with the method it sets up, and keep an experiment with its ablations. A printed subheading is evidence of internal structure, not a demand for another unit. Split a topic only at a meaningful turn, so that each part stands on its own. A short unit is right for a genuinely independent idea or a short conclusion; a run of sub-page units that all serve one question is not.

Cover the main text in order, without gaps or duplicate regions, and do not drop material. References are at most one optional navigation unit, and appendices at most one unit; neither is part of the main text.

Before answering, look at the whole map once more: does each unit answer exactly one question, does any title join two topics, and does every title say something specific about this paper?
