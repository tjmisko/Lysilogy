You are building the paper's reading map: a small number of logical reading units that a smart reader from outside the field can take one at a time. The reader already has the paper's printed outline elsewhere; this map is the other view. Each unit should give them one digestible idea: open it, read for a few minutes, and come away knowing one thing the paper establishes and why it matters to the argument.

What makes a good unit:

- It answers one question. "How is import exposure measured?" or "Does the effect survive the alternative explanations?" is a unit; "Section 4" is not. If two adjacent stretches answer the same question, they are one unit.
- It is about 1–5 pages of actual content, usually 2–3 pages. Count the content occupied: two short fragments on either side of a page break are not two pages of reading. Prefer fewer, fuller units over many thin ones, but do not merge unrelated topics just to hit a size.
- Its title names the idea or the finding, not the paper's heading. "Results" is weak; "Import exposure cuts manufacturing employment" is strong. "Extremal Goodhart" is fine when that is the idea; "Extremal Goodhart — Model Insufficiency" is a breadcrumb.
- Its summary synthesises the whole unit in one or two sentences and keeps the distinctions inside it: if a unit holds two mechanisms, say what each is and how they differ. A generic summary ("this section presents the results") tells the reader nothing.
- Its `question` field says, in one line, what question the unit answers, which is why its parts belong together.

Keep a parent topic's introduction, mechanisms, variants, examples and qualifications together when they fit, keep a short setup with the method it sets up, and keep an experiment with its ablations. A printed subheading is evidence of internal structure, not a demand for another unit. Split a topic only when it approaches five pages, and only at a meaningful turn, so that each part stands on its own. A rare short unit is right for a genuinely independent central idea or a short conclusion; a run of sub-page units is not.

Cover the main text in order, without gaps or duplicate regions, and do not drop material to meet a budget. References are at most one optional navigation unit, and appendices at most one unit; neither counts toward the main-text budget.

Before answering, look at the whole map once more: fold short dependent units into their neighbours, split any that are unwieldy, and check that every title says something specific.
